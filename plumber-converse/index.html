Where Converse water comes from

The City of Converse operates its own wells, named Bob Grubb, Gibbs Sprawl Rd and N Cimarron 2. They pump from the Edwards Aquifer, and the city also takes supply from Canyon Regional Water Authority (CRWA). The city uses both its own wells and CRWA supply, so the tap water you get is a blend of those sources.

For plumbing, the useful point is that treatment, filters and softeners work best when matched to what is actually in your supply. The 2025 water quality report lists chlorine residual averaging 1.12 ppm for the city, which is a disinfectant measure and not a hardness measure. No hardness figure appears in the 2025 reports we have seen, and we will not guess one.

So the first step is simple. Test your tap, and ask your utility for its annual water quality report. If you are shopping for treatment, learn what the report says about your supply before you buy. Our page on water softener and filtration explains how a plumber matches equipment to a test result.

Water heaters and mineral sediment

Tank water heaters collect sediment at the bottom over time, and the amount depends on the water they heat. Sediment makes a popping or rumbling sound, cuts efficiency and can overheat the tank bottom. General trade knowledge puts the life of a tank heater at roughly 8 to 12 years, and flushing the tank is a normal part of its upkeep.

Because Converse blends Edwards wells with CRWA water, we cannot say how much sediment a given heater will see. A flush and a look at the anode rod tell the plumber more than any city-wide guess. If your heater leaks from the tank itself, flushing will not help, and replacement is the honest answer.

A leak from the drain valve, a failed relief valve or a dead heating element is a different story, and a water heater repair can fix those without replacing the tank. The plumber checks which one it is and tells you the price before anything is done.

Water line breaks and low pressure

The line between the street meter and your house is yours to maintain on the house side. When it fails, you may see a wet patch in the yard, a meter that keeps running with everything off, or pressure that drops across the house. Find the main valve and close it to stop the water, then call.

A water line repair can mean spot repair of a small section or replacement of a longer run. The plumber locates the break first, and the price depends on length and access. If the problem is in the street, the utility handles it.

Drains and sewer backups

Not every Converse call is about supply water. A gurgling toilet, a slow kitchen sink or a tub that fills when the washer drains points to a clog. A plumber can clear it with a cable or jet, and our guide to clearing a clogged drain describes how the pipe is opened and tested. If every drain in the house backs up together, treat it as urgent and call.

Before the plumber arrives

1

Stop the supply

Close the main water valve at the meter or house. For a water heater leak, close the cold inlet valve on top of the heater.

2

Turn off the heater

For an electric heater, flip its breaker off. For gas, turn the control to pilot or off. If you smell gas, leave the building first, then call 911 and the gas utility from a safe distance. Do not touch the heater.

3

Check the meter

With all fixtures off, see whether the meter dial still moves, then tell the dispatcher.

4

Clear the area

Move belongings away from the heater, the pipe or the wet floor.
