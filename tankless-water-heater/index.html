How a tankless water heater works

A tankless heater has no storage tank. When a hot tap opens, a flow sensor feels water moving through the unit and tells the heater to start. In a gas unit, a burner lights and heats a metal heat exchanger. In an electric unit, heating elements switch on. The cold water passes through, picks up the heat, and leaves hot.

A control board watches the temperature of the water going in and out and adjusts the flame or power to hold the set temperature. When the tap closes, the flow stops and the heater shuts off. It does not burn fuel or draw power while no water is moving, apart from the electronics, which is the main source of savings compared with a tank.

That also explains the limits. The unit can heat only so much water per minute to a given temperature. Ask for more than it can deliver and the water gets cooler, which is why sizing matters more than with a tank.

Sizing by flow rate and temperature rise

Tankless units are sized by gallons per minute (GPM) and by temperature rise. Temperature rise is the difference between the cold water coming in and the temperature you want out. The cooler your incoming water, the harder the unit has to work, and the less flow it can deliver at that rise.

The method is simple. List the fixtures that could run at the same time, such as a shower, a kitchen faucet and a dishwasher. Add their flow rates. Then compare the total to the unit's rated flow at your temperature rise. If two showers and a washer might run together, the unit needs to cover that total.

We do not know your incoming water temperature from published local figures, so it is something to estimate with the plumber. Households that run many fixtures at once may use two smaller units or choose a larger one. A short sizing call with the dispatcher gets the right plumber sent out to measure instead of guess.

Gas line and venting for tankless

Gas tankless units burn more fuel at once than a tank, so the gas line often has to be larger. A line that fed a 40 gallon tank may be too small for a tankless unit that fires at a much higher rate. The plumber measures the run, adds up the other gas appliances and checks the sizing against the unit's manual.

Venting is different too. Many tankless units are high efficiency and make cool exhaust that needs special vent pipe, such as stainless steel or polypropylene, and sometimes a condensate drain. Always check the unit's manual, because the right vent material, length and termination are set by the manufacturer.

Gas work needs a licensed plumber. Do not attempt a gas line change yourself. If you ever smell gas, leave the building, then call 911 and the gas utility from a safe distance. The City of Cibolo enforces the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC), and permit rules vary, so ask the City of Cibolo building department about your job.

Electric tankless and the load on your panel

An electric tankless unit draws a very large load for a few seconds at a time. It usually needs one or more dedicated large circuits and heavy wire, and an older panel may not have the room. That can turn a simple swap into an electrical upgrade.

Wiring is electrical work, and we do not do it. A plumber handles the water side and an electrician handles the circuits. Check the unit's label for its amp draw and compare it with your panel before you buy.

In many homes, a gas unit or a tank heater avoids this problem. Electric tankless units suit small loads such as a bathroom or a point-of-use spot, though whole-house electric units exist for homes with the electrical capacity.

Scale, descaling and flushing

Minerals in water can build up as scale on the heat exchanger. Scale acts like insulation, so the unit has to work harder, makes noise, runs hot and eventually throws error codes. A tankless unit has narrow passages, so even a thin layer of scale matters more than in a tank.

The fix is a flush. The plumber connects a small pump and hoses to service valves, called isolation valves, on the unit, and circulates a descaling solution for a set time. White vinegar is often mentioned as a general-knowledge option, but the manufacturer's manual names the approved solution and the steps. Follow the manual.

How often to flush depends on the water and the unit. No hardness figure appears in the 2025 reports we have seen, so we do not state one. You can test your tap with a simple kit, and your water utility publishes an annual water quality report that you can ask for. Between the test and the manual, you can set a schedule. If your unit has no isolation valves, the plumber can add them during a service call.

Error codes and no hot water

Tankless units show a code on a display when something is off. Codes differ by brand, so the manual is the key.

The common faults fall into groups. An ignition fault means the burner did not light, which can come from gas supply, a dirty igniter or a vent problem. A flame rod fault means the unit cannot sense the flame. A flow sensor fault means the unit does not feel enough water moving. A condensate fault means the drain for a high efficiency unit is blocked.

Before calling, check the simple things. Make sure the gas valve is open, the power is on, and the vent is clear. Then check for a clogged inlet screen or a closed isolation valve. If the code returns, a plumber can read it and test the part.

A plumber can also tell you if the unit needs a flush, a part, or replacement. Fixing a faulty unit is covered under water heater repair, and the choice to replace is explained on the water heater replacement page.

The cold water sandwich and minimum flow

A tankless heater needs a minimum flow of water to fire, often around half a gallon per minute, though the exact number is in the manual. If you open a tap just a little, the unit may not start, or may start and stop. This is the minimum flow issue.

The cold water sandwich is a related effect. If you use hot water, shut off the tap briefly and then reopen it, the hot water in the pipe has cooled and you get a slug of cold before the heat returns. Long pipe runs between the heater and the tap make this worse.

Fixes include a recirculation line, a small buffer tank or moving the heater closer to the fixtures. A plumber can tell you what fits the layout of your house.

Tankless or tank, the honest trade-offs

Tankless units give hot water on demand and take less space. They can last longer than a tank, with general trade guidance of around 15 to 20 years or more with regular flushing, compared with roughly 8 to 12 years for a tank. They also use no fuel while idle.

The costs are real. The unit costs more upfront, the gas line, vent or electrical work can add to it, and the unit needs routine flushing. A tank is simpler, cheaper to buy and easier to repair, though it stores hot water that cools and can run out. If your household uses small amounts at different times, a tank may suit you. If you run many fixtures, a tankless unit may suit you better.
