How to Fix a Leaky Kitchen Faucet: Cibolo, TX Guide

A dripping kitchen faucet wastes water all day and wears a stain into the sink. Most drips come from a worn rubber part that costs little to replace, and many homeowners can do the job in an afternoon with basic tools.

This guide shows how to read the drip, fix a cartridge or compression faucet, and recognize the point where a plumber is the safer choice.

Where is the drip coming from?

Before you buy parts, watch the faucet for a few minutes. The location of the leak tells you which part is worn, and it saves you from replacing the wrong one.

A drip from the tip of the spout means the valve inside is not sealing when it closes. In a compression faucet that is a worn washer or a pitted seat. In a cartridge faucet, it is a worn cartridge or damaged seals.

Water that seeps out around the base of the spout points to worn O-rings where the spout swivels. Water that leaks around the handle points to a loose packing nut, a worn stem seal or a cracked cartridge body.

A leak that only shows up when the sprayer is used often points to the diverter or the sprayer hose, not the main valve. And water that appears in the cabinet below is a separate problem. It may be a supply line, a shutoff valve or the faucet itself, so check it carefully before assuming anything.

Tools and parts to gather first

You do not need a large kit. Most kitchen faucet repairs use a small set of tools and one replacement part.

  • Adjustable wrench and a set of screwdrivers, flathead and Phillips
  • Hex keys, since many handles hide a small set screw
  • Needle-nose pliers for pulling out clips and old cartridges
  • Plumber's grease for new O-rings and seals
  • A rag, a small bowl and a towel to protect the sink
  • White vinegar and an old toothbrush to clean mineral deposits

Identify the faucet before buying a cartridge. Brand and model are often stamped on the underside of the body or printed on the box if you kept it. If you cannot find them, take the old cartridge or washer to the hardware store and match it by hand. Cartridges from different brands look alike but do not interchange, so a close match is not good enough.

Step by step: replacing a cartridge or washer

The sequence below fits the two most common designs. A compression faucet has two handles that you screw down to close. A cartridge faucet has one lever or two handles that turn a sealed insert. The steps share the same first half.

1

Shut off the water

Close the two shutoff valves under the sink by turning them clockwise. Open the faucet to release pressure and confirm the flow stops.

2

Plug the drain

Place a rag or stopper in the sink drain so a dropped screw or clip does not disappear.

3

Remove the handle

Pry off the decorative cap, remove the screw underneath, then lift the handle off. Some have a set screw on the side.

4

Take out the old part

Unscrew the retaining nut or clip. Pull the cartridge straight up, or remove the stem and washer from a compression faucet.

Once the old part is out, look at it closely. A flattened washer, a cracked rubber seal or a scored brass seat explains the leak. Compare the new part against the old one before installing it. They should match in size, shape and the number of ports or notches.

Wipe the inside of the faucet body with a rag and rinse out any grit. Coat the new O-rings lightly in plumber's grease, which helps them seat and slide without tearing. Drop the new cartridge or washer in the same orientation as the old one. Most cartridges have a tab or flat side that must line up with a slot, and forcing the wrong orientation can crack the body.

Reassemble in reverse order, tightening the retaining nut snugly but not hard. Over-tightening crushes the seals and makes the handle stiff. Put the handle back, then restore the water slowly by opening the shutoff valves. Open the faucet and let it run for a minute with the aerator removed so any debris flushes out. Then screw the aerator back on and check for drips at the spout, the handle and the base.

Cleaning the aerator and sprayer

If the stream is weak or sprays sideways, the aerator may be clogged with sediment. Unscrew it from the tip of the spout, take it apart and soak the pieces in white vinegar for an hour. Scrub the screen gently with a toothbrush and rinse, then reassemble.

A sprayer that drips from the head or leaks under the sink often has a worn hose washer or a cracked hose. Replacement hoses are inexpensive and screw on by hand or with a wrench. If the diverter inside the spout sticks and sends water to the sprayer when you want the stream, cleaning or replacing it usually solves it.

When the faucet is not worth fixing

Repairs make sense for a faucet in good shape that has one worn part. They make less sense when the whole faucet is failing. A faucet with a pitted or corroded body, a finish that is flaking off, or a leak that returns within months is telling you it is at the end of its life. General trade knowledge puts many faucets at roughly 10 to 15 years of service, depending on use and finish.

Consider replacement if parts for your model are discontinued, if you have already replaced the cartridge once, or if you want a different style. A faucet swap is usually a straightforward job for a plumber and may cost less in total than a string of repairs on an old one. You can read more about the options on the faucet repair page.

When to stop and call a licensed plumber

DIY is a fair choice for a drip, but there are clear stopping points. If you hit one of them, put the tools down.

Stop if the shutoff valves under the sink will not close fully or if they leak when you turn them. Old shutoffs corrode and can break when forced, which turns a drip into a flood. If water keeps flowing with the valves closed, shut off the main water valve for the house and call a plumber.

Stop if a nut or part is seized and will not turn under reasonable force. Corroded fittings can snap, and a broken fitting inside the faucet body often means the whole faucet has to come out.

Stop if you find water under the sink that is not coming from the faucet. A leak in a supply line or the drain trap, or a hidden leak inside the cabinet wall, needs a proper diagnosis, which is part of what leak detection does.

Stop, too, if you smell gas. Leave the building first, then call 911 and the gas utility from a safe distance. If water has reached an outlet or you see damaged wiring near the sink, turn off power at the breaker only if the panel is dry, otherwise stay away and call.

Look at your water bill to see which name is on it. That matters because each utility publishes its own annual water quality report.

That matters for your repair. If buildup is truly heavy, a cartridge may clog or wear faster, and soaking parts in vinegar at each repair helps. If testing shows the water is not the cause, look instead at old parts, loose fittings or an aerator that was never cleaned.
