What goes wrong along the I-35 corridor

A busy commercial strip puts plumbing under a kind of stress that a quiet street never sees. Restaurant kitchens push grease into drains all day. Restrooms in shops and event venues take sudden surges when a crowd arrives. Water lines under parking lots get driven over every hour.

The calls that follow are fairly predictable. A floor drain backs up behind a kitchen. A toilet in a shared restroom runs and floods the tile. A tenant finds water on the ceiling and nobody knows which unit it came from. In each case the first useful thing is knowing how many tenants share the pipe, because that decides how far the water can travel and who has to be told.

Houses in the same town fail in quieter ways, and they get the same attention. A water heater weeping at the base, a toilet that will not stop running or a kitchen sink that stops draining all fit a standard home call.

Restaurants, shops and shared buildings

Retama Park and The Forum at Olympia Parkway shopping center are both in Selma, and they point to a kind of building where plumbing is shared. If you run a food business, a restaurant, bar or snack counter near an event venue, plan for the busy weekend rather than the quiet Tuesday. A slow floor drain or a restroom that gurgles is a warning that the line will not survive a crowd.

Many food businesses have a grease interceptor, which is a tank that catches fats before they enter the sewer. If it is not pumped on schedule, the grease slips past and coats the drain pipe downstream. A visit to clear the drain can open the line, but a plumber will usually ask when the interceptor was last emptied, since the cable only treats the symptom.

In a building with several tenants, one main drain usually serves many units. When it clogs, the lowest drain gives way first, and that tenant gets the flooding even if the blockage began three doors up. Our commercial plumbing work page covers the work that suits shops, restaurants and multi-tenant buildings, including why the landlord and tenants should agree beforehand on who calls.

Water in a ceiling or a wall

Water that appears on a ceiling, drips from a light fitting or darkens a wall has traveled from somewhere above or behind. In a one-story house that is often a supply line or the water heater closet. In a two-story shop or an upstairs apartment it could be the unit overhead.

Shut off the water to the building at the main valve, and if the leak stops, you know it is a pressurized supply line. If it keeps dripping, it may be a drain or a roof issue, and a plumber can tell the difference. When the source is hidden, electronic leak detection uses listening gear and moisture readings to narrow the spot before anyone opens drywall.

Keep people and equipment away from wet electrical areas. If water is dripping near a light, outlet or breaker panel, do not touch it, and say so on the call. A plumber does not replace an electrician, and we will tell you when the electrical side needs its own visit.
