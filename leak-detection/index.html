Finding a hidden water leak in Cibolo, TX

Leak detection in Cibolo, TX means locating a leak in a pipe you cannot see, whether it runs under a slab, inside a wall, above a ceiling or beneath the yard. A visible drip under a sink is a repair. A hidden leak is a search problem first, and the answer to that search decides how much of your house has to be opened.

Water does not stay where it escapes. It follows the pipe, runs along framing and soaks into insulation or soil before it shows up as a stain or a soft patch. So the wet spot you notice is often several feet from the actual hole, and cutting there usually finds nothing.

That is the reason to pay for finding the leak first. A pinhole in a pressurized copper line can lose a surprising amount of water over a month, and the bill is the first thing to move. The sooner the search starts, the less damage the drywall, flooring and framing have to absorb.

Signs that water is escaping where you cannot see it

Most hidden leaks announce themselves in more than one way. One sign alone can have a boring cause, but two or three together point to a leak in the pipes.

Bill jumps with no change at home

Water passes the meter all day and night, so the charge climbs even when nobody is using more.

Running water sound in a quiet house

A hiss or trickle at night, when every fixture is closed, often means pressurized water is escaping from a pipe.

Warm patch on the floor

A hot water line leaking under a floor can heat the surface above it. Bare feet notice it before eyes do.

Musty smell or visible mold

Damp drywall, carpet or insulation grows mold in days, and the smell shows up before any stain.

Low or uneven water pressure

A leak diverts flow before it reaches the fixtures, so pressure drops at the far end of the house.

Paint bubbling, stains or soft flooring

Moisture is working through a wall or ceiling surface from behind.

None of these proves a leak. A toilet flapper or a running irrigation zone can produce the same bill. That is why the first step is to rule out the simple causes with the meter, which is covered a little further down the page.

How a plumber finds a leak without tearing things open

Leak detection is a set of methods, and a good visit uses more than one. Each method answers a different question, and none of them is magic. The plumber picks the order based on what you describe.

Electronic acoustic listening is the workhorse. A sensitive microphone or ground sensor picks up the hiss or vibration that water makes as it escapes a pressurized pipe. The plumber moves along the suspected line and listens for the loudest point. Metal pipe carries sound well, while plastic pipe carries it poorly, which is one reason results vary.

Pressure testing isolates a line. The plumber closes valves to separate one section, such as the hot side or a branch to a bathroom, applies pressure and watches the gauge. If the needle falls, that section leaks. If it holds, the leak is somewhere else, and the search narrows by elimination.

Thermal imaging reads surface temperature. A camera shows cool or warm patterns that can mean moisture in a wall, ceiling or floor, or a hot line leaking under a slab. A moisture meter then confirms the reading on the surface. Thermal images suggest, and the meter and the listening gear confirm.

In some cases a plumber uses tracer gas, a harmless gas mix pushed into an emptied line that escapes at the leak and is picked up by a sensor above it. It helps on stubborn leaks when listening fails. It adds equipment and time, so it is not the first tool.

Honest limits of leak detection

No method finds every leak every time. Background noise from traffic or an air conditioner can mask the sound of a small leak. Deep or insulated pipe dulls a signal. A leak that only runs when a pump or zone valve is on can look quiet during the test.

When the first pass is inconclusive, the plumber tells you so, and the next move is another method, not a guess. Ask what was ruled out and what was found. A clear answer matters more than a confident one, and you hear the price before work starts.

The water meter check, in a few minutes

You can learn a lot before anyone arrives. Close every tap, stop the dishwasher and washer, and make sure nobody flushes. Then look at the low-flow indicator on your meter. If it keeps turning, water is moving somewhere between the meter and your fixtures.

Next, close your home's main shutoff valve and look again. If the indicator stops, the leak is on the house side of that valve. If it keeps turning, the leak is on the line between the meter and the valve. That one fact tells the plumber which part of the property to start with.

Leaks in ceilings and walls

A stain on a ceiling is the classic emergency, because water collecting above drywall can bring down a section without warning. If the ceiling sags or bulges, keep people out from under it.

The source is above the stain. It may be a supply line, a drain line, a shower pan, an air conditioner condensate line or a roof leak that only looks like a plumbing leak. A plumber separates these by pressure testing the supply lines and checking whether the stain grows when no one is using water or when it has not rained.

Wall leaks are harder to read because water can travel down inside the wall cavity and show up near the floor. A moisture meter and a thermal image trace the wet area upward, and the plumber opens the smallest access hole that reaches the pipe.

Under the house and crawlspace leaks

If your home has a crawlspace or a raised floor, supply and drain pipes often run underneath where you cannot see them. A leak there may show as damp insulation, a sagging subfloor, a musty smell or a floor that feels soft near a wall. If your home sits on a slab instead, those same lines run in or under the concrete, and the work looks different, as described on the slab leak repair page.

In a crawlspace, the plumber can often see and hear the pipe directly. Dripping, mineral crusting on joints and wet insulation point to the spot. Wear protective gear if you go down yourself, and do not enter if the space is flooded and there are electrical lines near the water.

Buried yard lines and the line from the meter

Between the meter and the house, a buried line carries all of your water. A leak here can run for a long time because the water simply soaks into the ground. Signs include a wet or unusually green strip in the lawn, a soft spot with no rain, a low point that stays muddy, or a meter that runs with the main valve closed.

Finding it is a mix of listening at the meter box and at valves along the likely route, then pressure checks on the line. Once the spot is found, the repair is covered under water line repair, and the plumber explains whether the fix is a short section or a longer run.

Buried utility lines can run near a service line, so do not start digging on your own. Ask the plumber or your utility about having buried lines marked. Who owns which piece of the service line depends on the utility, so ask yours before assuming a repair is your cost or theirs.

Irrigation leaks

Sprinkler systems leak quietly. A cracked riser, a broken head, a bad valve diaphragm or a split lateral can run during every cycle and add up to a high bill. Signs include soggy patches, geysers at the head, low pressure in a zone and a valve that never fully closes.

To separate irrigation from the house plumbing, turn the controller off and shut the irrigation supply valve, then check the meter again. If the indicator stops, the leak is on the irrigation side. Many irrigation lines are shallow, so repairs are often a short dig and a new fitting.

Multi-unit and commercial property leaks

Rental buildings, small offices and shopping units add a few wrinkles. Water usually runs through shared risers, so a leak in one unit can show up as a stain in the unit below, and the cause may be in a wall that belongs to neither tenant. The plumber isolates risers and branches with valves, then tests each section.

If you manage a building, tell the dispatcher how many units share the line and where the main shutoff is. Commercial properties may also have backflow devices, fire lines or larger meters that call for their own approach. Describe the layout on the call and the plumber plans from there. Larger commercial work can also fall under commercial plumbing.

Pool and spa plumbing

We should be straightforward here. A leak in the plumbing lines that serve a pool or spa, such as the supply line, the return line or the equipment pad, is something a plumber can often test and locate. A leak in the pool shell itself, the liner or the structure, is a different specialty that belongs to pool contractors.

If you are not sure which one you have, say so when you call and ask the dispatcher whether your situation fits. That saves you a visit that cannot solve the problem.

When a hidden leak is an emergency

Treat a leak as an emergency when water is on a ceiling, near a breaker panel or outlet, spreading across a finished floor or running with no way to stop it at a fixture. Shut off the main valve first, then call. Our line is open nights, weekends and holidays, and a licensed plumber is dispatched for same-day appointments when available.

A slow leak that you can control by closing the main valve can often wait for a daytime visit, but waiting still costs. The longer drywall and subfloor stay wet, the more likely mold and rot become. Calling the same day, even if the appointment is the next morning, is almost always cheaper than watching it.
