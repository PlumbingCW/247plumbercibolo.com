What the water service line is

The water service line is the buried pipe that carries drinking water from the utility's meter to your house. It is separate from the plumbing inside the walls. In Cibolo, TX, as anywhere, it runs underground for a stretch of the yard, passes through or under the foundation and connects to the main shutoff valve inside or just outside the house.

Because it is buried, a leak can go on for a long time before anyone sees it. The soil absorbs the water, and the first sign is often a high bill, a patch of grass that stays green in a dry week or a house where the tap pressure has quietly dropped.

The main supply is the first stretch of pipe after the line enters the house, up to the branches that feed the fixtures. When people say the "main water line," they sometimes mean one, sometimes the other, and the plumber will ask which. It matters for who owns the repair.

Signs of a water line problem

Low pressure at every tap

If showers and sinks are all weak at once, the leak or restriction is probably before the branches split, in the main or the service line.

Wet or sunken patch in the yard

Soft ground, a puddle with no rain or a depression along the line from the meter to the house points to a buried leak.

Meter moving with everything off

If the low-flow indicator keeps turning with every tap closed, water is escaping somewhere after the meter.

Cloudy or brown water

Disturbed sediment, rust or a break that lets soil in can colour the water. Run a cold tap for a few minutes and see if it clears.

Hissing or running sound

A pressurized leak makes noise, often loudest near the main valve or where the line enters the house.

No water at all

A closed valve, a failed line, a frozen or broken pipe or a utility outage can all stop the flow. Check the meter box and ask the neighbors.

If several of these appear together, treat it as a leak until the meter test says otherwise. A full test is explained in the steps below.

When no water is an emergency

A house with no water is urgent when you cannot flush, cook or wash, when a baby or an older relative lives there, or when water is visibly pooling and eating into the yard or driveway. Do not wait to see whether it comes back.

Before you call, run the simple checks.

Look at the meter box to see whether the meter is turning, and ask a neighbor whether their water is on. If the whole street is dry, it is a utility problem and the right call is your water utility. If only your house is dry, the cause is on your side and our line is open nights, weekends and holidays. Why water lines fail

These are general causes and not a claim about any one Cibolo house. Pipe ages and wears out. Old galvanized steel rusts shut from the inside, and some older homes still have lead service lines that the owner may want replaced. Copper can pit, plastic can crack at a fitting and joints can pull apart.

Ground moves too. Soil shrinks in a dry spell and swells after rain, and a pipe that cannot flex takes the strain at a joint. Tree roots follow moisture and can wrap or press a line. Another cause is damage during digging, such as a fence post hole, a trench for a new irrigation system or a landscaping job that nicked the pipe.

Pressure is the amplifier. A line that holds normal pressure for years can start to weep when pressure climbs after a failed reducing valve. The slow weep then wears a bigger hole as the pressurized water cuts into the pipe and washes out the soil around it.

Finding the leak before anyone digs

The plumber starts with a meter test. With every tap and appliance off, a moving meter dial means water is escaping after the meter. Closing the main shutoff valve then tells you which side the leak is on. If the meter keeps moving with the house valve closed, the leak is between the meter and that valve, which is the service line.

Next the plumber listens. Acoustic equipment, such as ground microphones and a sensor on the line, picks up the sound of pressurized water leaking and narrows the spot to a few feet. Pressure testing a section isolates which stretch holds and which does not. A utility locator maps where the line actually runs, since old lines do not always follow a straight path from meter to house.

Honest limits apply. A very small leak, a plastic pipe that muffles sound or a spot under a slab can be harder to pinpoint, and the plumber says so rather than guessing. You hear that before any ground is opened.

Repair or replace the line

A single clean break in an otherwise sound pipe can usually be repaired by cutting out the damaged section and putting in a new piece with proper fittings. That is the smaller job. If the pipe is corroded along its length, has failed more than once or is made of material the owner wants gone, replacement of the whole line is often the better value, because each repair is another dig.

Trenchless or open-cut, an honest comparison

Open-cut means digging a trench along the line, pulling out the old pipe and laying new pipe. It is the most direct method, it lets the plumber see every inch of the pipe and the soil, and it works almost anywhere. The cost is a torn-up yard, and any driveway, sidewalk or landscaping in the path has to be restored.

Trenchless methods dig only at the two ends. In pipe bursting, a tool pulls a new pipe through the old one and breaks the old one apart as it goes. In pipe lining, a resin liner is cured inside the existing pipe. Both disturb far less of the yard and leave a driveway intact.

Trenchless has limits. The old pipe has to follow a path that a tool can travel, a badly collapsed line may not allow it, and not every plumber or line suits it. It also may not be the cheaper option, because the equipment is specialised. Ask the plumber whether your line can be done without a trench, and what the trade-offs are for your layout.

Shutoff valves and who owns what

Two valves matter. The meter valve sits at the meter box and belongs to the utility. Do not turn it yourself, because the utility does not want customers operating its equipment, and a damaged valve can leave a whole connection without water. If you think the meter valve needs to close, call your water utility.

The second is the main shutoff on your side, usually where the line enters the house or at the edge of the foundation. That one is yours. Learn where it is and turn it a few times a year so it does not seize. A round handle turns clockwise to close, and a lever handle turns a quarter turn.

Responsibility for the line itself depends on the utility and where the break is. Often the utility owns up to and including the meter and the homeowner owns the pipe on the house side, but practice differs, so ask your water utility before assuming. Call before repair work starts, not after.

Pressure and the pressure reducing valve

Water mains can run at higher pressure than home plumbing is meant to take. Many houses have a pressure reducing valve, a bell-shaped brass fitting near the main shutoff, that drops incoming pressure to a safer working level. Over time its internal parts wear, and the valve can fail in either direction.

A failed valve that lets full street pressure through can stress every fitting in the house, shorten the life of the water heater and washing machine hoses and make faucets and toilets act up. Banging pipes, a relief valve that drips on the water heater or repeated leaks at fittings point toward high pressure. A plumber puts a gauge on a hose bib to read the actual pressure, then decides whether the valve can be adjusted or needs replacing.

If the pressure is low instead, the plumber checks the valve, the main shutoff and the line itself for scale, a partly closed valve or a leak.
