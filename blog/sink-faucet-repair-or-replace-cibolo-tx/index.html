Sink Faucet: Repair or Replace? A Cibolo, TX Guide

A faucet that drips or sticks does not always need to be thrown out, and one that still works is not always worth saving. The right call depends on age, parts, finish and what is going on inside the body.

This guide gives you a way to decide, then covers the basics of swapping one yourself and the point where a plumber is the safer choice.

Repair or replace: the quick test

Start with one question. Is the faucet failing in one part, or is the whole thing tired? A single worn cartridge, washer or O-ring is a small repair on a faucet that is otherwise sound. Several things failing at once, or the same part failing over and over, tells you the faucet has reached the end of its useful life.

Faucets are mechanical parts with seals that wear, and the metal body wears too. Most last many years, though the range is wide and depends on the build, the use and the water. The framework below weighs the main signals.

Age and the cost of keeping a faucet alive

There is no single age at which a faucet must go, but there is a pattern. A faucet that has run for a decade or more has had its seals flexed a great many times. Cartridges are cheap compared with a new faucet, yet each repair costs time. If you have already replaced the cartridge twice, a third repair is likely to be a short-term fix.

Think in terms of time and parts, not just price. A cartridge swap needs a part that fits your exact model, a way to shut off the water, and about the time it takes to take the faucet apart and put it back together. If the model is discontinued, the part search alone can take longer than buying a new faucet. Our faucet repair page explains what is usually fixable.

Finish damage and corrosion

A scratched or flaking finish does not get better. Plated finishes on cheaper faucets can peel and expose the base metal. Refinishing is rarely practical for a household faucet, so a worn finish tends to push the decision toward replacement.

Corrosion deserves more attention than looks. White or green crust around the base, a damp cabinet floor, or a handle that is hard to turn suggests water is working its way into places it should not be. If the crust is only on the surface, cleaning may help. If it goes deep, or the base rocks, the body is compromised.

Low flow is another corrosion clue. If the aerator is clean and flow is still weak, mineral buildup or corrosion inside the faucet passages is likely, and cleaning them rarely lasts. Replacement is usually the better route.

Kitchen, bathroom and laundry sinks differ

A kitchen faucet works hardest. It runs many times a day, often with a pull-down hose and a spray head that adds more parts to wear out. Hose connections under the sink are a common leak point, and they are worth checking before blaming the faucet.

Bathroom faucets see lighter use, but they are often paired with a pop-up drain assembly and tight cabinet space. Small, widespread and centerset faucets differ in how they mount, and that affects what you can swap in.

Laundry and utility sinks often have simple two-handle faucets with a threaded spout, and a more basic build. They tolerate rough use, and replacement is usually straightforward as long as the deck and hole spacing match. In many cases a laundry faucet that leaks is cheaper to replace than to rebuild.

Replacing a faucet yourself: the basics

A faucet swap is a manageable DIY job for many people, if the shutoffs work and the sink is easy to reach.

1

Shut off the water

Close the two angle stops under the sink. If they will not turn or leak when turned, stop and consider replacing them first, or shut the main valve.

2

Check the sink

Count the mounting holes and measure the deck thickness. A single-hole faucet can fit a three-hole sink with a cover plate, but a three-hole faucet will not fit a single hole.

3

Disconnect and remove

Open the tap to relieve pressure, disconnect the supply lines and loosen the mounting nuts with a basin wrench, which reaches up behind the sink.

4

Mount and connect

Set the new faucet, tighten the nuts, connect new supply lines, run the water and check every connection for drips.

New supply lines are cheap and worth fitting. Old braided lines and rubber washers can fail silently inside a cabinet. Hand-tighten, then give a small turn with a wrench. Overtightening cracks washers and damages fittings.

When to replace the angle stops

The small shutoff valves under the sink, called angle stops, are easy to overlook. They are the reason you can work on a faucet without turning off the whole house. Old ones seize, drip from the stem, or fail to close all the way.

If a stop will not turn, do not force it. A frozen stem can snap and flood the cabinet. If it drips around the handle or does not fully shut off flow, replace it when you replace the faucet, since you will already have the water off. Fitting new stops takes a plumber a little extra time, and it prevents a bigger problem later.

When to call a plumber

Call a licensed plumber if the stops will not close, the supply lines are corroded or crimped, or you find water damage under the sink. Call too if the faucet is part of a unit you cannot reach, such as a wall-mounted or commercial-style fixture. A leak you cannot stop is an urgent call, and shutting the main valve buys time.

Be careful near electrical items. If water is near outlets, the garbage disposal power or a dishwasher, turn off the breaker for that area before reaching into the cabinet. If a pipe under the sink is damaged, or a fitting breaks, a new fixture is not the whole job, and the plumber may suggest replacing the fixtures and shutoffs together.
